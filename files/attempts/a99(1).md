# Relational Order Spaces

### A Formal Theory of Computation-Avoiding Comparison for Arithmetic Expressions — Capabilities, Hard Limits, and Connections to PosSLP

**A response to the research note "Beyond Scalar Decomposition" by Hung Dinh Phu Dang (17–22 June 2026)**

---

## Executive Summary

The original note asks a sharp question: instead of reducing N expressions to N scalars and comparing the scalars, can we build a mathematical space in which expressions are *directly* comparable — carrying their own built-in notion of order and "distance" — so that the act of full numeric computation becomes unnecessary?

This is a real and important question. It is not new, but it has not been *solved* in the sense the note hopes for, and it turns out to be equivalent to one of the most stubborn open problems in theoretical computer science: **PosSLP**, the question of whether the sign of a number produced by a short arithmetic program can be determined in polynomial time. This equivalence is provable, not speculative, and it is the central result of this paper.

The good news is threefold:

1. The note's intuition is *correct* in a precise, citable sense — ordinary scalar evaluation is **not** the cheap O(n) baseline it appears to be, for exactly the reason the note's own stress test (1000 × 1000 vs. unary blow-up) identifies.
2. A rigorous "expression-native" comparison space — we will call it a **Relational Order Space (ROS)** — can be built, and it already exists in deployed form inside computational geometry libraries (LEDA, CORE, CGAL), where it produces large, measured real-world speedups over naïve scalar evaluation.
3. The space has a precise, provable boundary. Beyond that boundary lies a 20-year-old open problem in complexity theory. This paper identifies exactly where that boundary is, why it is there, and what would need to be true for it to be crossed.

What follows is the formal treatment: definitions, theorems, a stress-test self-check against the note's own examples, an honest accounting of what the framework can and cannot deliver, and a concrete architecture for implementing it on ordinary binary hardware — which directly answers the note's closing question.

## 1. Restating the Question with Mathematical Precision

The note's "Expressions" must first be made precise, or no theorem can be stated about them.

**Definition 1.1 (Expression).** An *expression* is a directed acyclic graph (DAG) whose leaves are rational constants (the note's "finite-precision real numbers" are, for any fixed precision, rational numbers — a decimal with finitely many digits is a fraction) and whose internal nodes are labeled by one of {+, −, ×, ÷}, each with the appropriate number of inputs drawn from earlier nodes or leaves. One node is designated the *output*.

This is precisely the standard notion of an **arithmetic circuit**, also called a **straight-line program (SLP)** when written as a sequence of instructions rather than drawn as a graph. The note's insistence on "structural integrity — no decomposition into scalars" is, formally, the insistence on working with the DAG itself rather than with the rational number it denotes. This is exactly the object complexity theorists already study for exactly the reason the note gives: the DAG can be exponentially smaller than the number it produces.

**Definition 1.2 (Size).** The *size* of an expression is its number of nodes — what the note calls N, the count of operations. This is the only complexity parameter we are allowed to use if we want results "driven by structure, not by the blown-up value."

**Definition 1.3 (Value).** Each expression E denotes a value val(E) ∈ ℚ (or "undefined" if a division by zero occurs along the way). This is the "scalar" the note wants to avoid computing.

**The Comparison Problem (informal restatement of the note's core question).** Given two expressions A and B, decide whether val(A) < val(B), val(A) = val(B), or val(A) > val(B) — using an algorithm whose cost is governed by the *sizes* of A and B, not by the *bit-length* of val(A) or val(B).

This is the object this paper studies. It is, word for word, the note's research question made formal.

## 2. Decidability vs. Efficiency: An Important Clarification

The note lists "Decidability" as a requirement twice — once paired with "polynomial-time bounds," once as "rational trade-offs preventing theoretical vacuousness." It is worth separating these, because they are not the same question, and conflating them obscures where the real difficulty lives.

For expressions built only from {+, −, ×, ÷} over rational constants (Definition 1.1), **decidability is not in doubt and never was.** Every such expression denotes an exact, finite rational number (or is undefined), computable in finitely many steps by direct evaluation. There is no algorithmic barrier to deciding val(A) vs. val(B) — you can always just compute both and subtract.

This is precisely *why* the note is right to exclude transcendental functions. **Richardson's theorem** (Richardson, 1968) shows that once you allow expressions built from sin, exp, π, and ln 2 in addition to +, ×, and composition, the question "is this expression identically zero?" becomes **recursively undecidable** — no algorithm can answer it for all such expressions, for any cost. The note's instinct to stop at {+, −, ×, ÷} is exactly the boundary that keeps the theory decidable: this is the fragment covered by the **Tarski–Seidenberg theorem**, which guarantees that the full first-order theory of the real numbers under +, ×, and order is decidable. Richardson's result is precisely what breaks the moment you add sin or exp on top of that field structure.

So: decidability is free, granted, and already known — it is a consequence of working inside the real closed field, not a new contribution this framework needs to supply. **The entire open content of the note's question is about efficiency**: can the decision be made using resources proportional to expression *size*, rather than to the *bit-length of the value*? That is a single, sharp, well-posed question, and the rest of this paper is devoted to it.

## 3. This Problem Already Has a Name

It would be a disservice to treat the note's question as occurring in a vacuum. The exact question — compare expressions via their structure, without paying for the size of the value — has been studied under specific names for several decades, in two adjacent traditions: theoretical computer science (where it is a famous *open* problem) and computational geometry (where a *practical*, partial solution is already deployed in production software).

### 3.1 PosSLP: the canonical formalization

**Definition 3.1 (PosSLP).** Given a division-free straight-line program that computes an integer N, decide whether N > 0.

This problem was introduced by Allender, Bürgisser, Kjeldgaard-Pedersen, and Miltersen in *On the Complexity of Numerical Analysis* (CCC 2006; SIAM J. Computing 2009) as the single bottleneck underlying two seemingly unrelated open questions in numerical analysis: the complexity of the Blum–Shub–Smale real computation model, and the complexity of standard floating-point numerical computation. They proved that **PosSLP lies in the Counting Hierarchy** (a class sitting inside PSPACE) — a real, nontrivial upper bound, far below the exponential blow-up of literal evaluation. They also showed that the Euclidean Traveling Salesman decision problem reduces to PosSLP, putting a problem of obvious practical importance on the same footing.

What they did **not** show, and what nobody has shown in the twenty years since, is that PosSLP is in P. It is widely believed to be hard, and it is tied to other long-standing open problems, including the complexity of polynomial identity testing.

Notice what PosSLP already is: it is *the question of the sign of a number, asked directly about the program that produces it, without ever requiring the program to be unrolled into the number itself.* It is, formally, "Expressions → Relational Structure → Direct Analysis" — the note's proposed approach is not a new method sitting outside this literature; it is, when made precise, **this exact, named, actively studied problem.**

### 3.2 Extending to comparison and division

The note's expressions allow division; PosSLP as stated does not. The standard technique (used by Allender et al. themselves) is to push every division gate to the top of the circuit, so that an arbitrary expression's value can always be written as v = v₁ / v₂, where v₁ and v₂ are division-free SLPs. Determining the sign of v then costs two PosSLP queries (the signs of v₁ and v₂); comparing two such expressions A and B reduces, by cross-multiplication, to a bounded number of further PosSLP queries on circuits of polynomial size in the originals.

**Theorem 3.2 (Equivalence Theorem).** The Comparison Problem of §1 is polynomial-time equivalent to PosSLP.

*Proof sketch.* (⊆, upper bound) By the division-clearing reduction above, any instance of the Comparison Problem can be answered using a constant number of calls to a PosSLP oracle on circuits of size polynomial in |A| + |B|. Since PosSLP ∈ Counting Hierarchy ⊆ PSPACE, and PSPACE is closed under polynomial-time oracle access to itself, the Comparison Problem lies in PSPACE (more precisely, within the same level of the Counting Hierarchy as PosSLP, up to the standard closure properties of that hierarchy).

(⊇, lower bound) PosSLP itself is the special case B = 0: given any division-free SLP computing N, ask the Comparison Problem whether that expression is greater than the trivial expression "0." This is a polynomial-time (in fact linear-time) reduction. ∎

This theorem is the central technical fact of this paper. It says: **the note's research question is not "is there a faster way to compare expressions than evaluating them" in the abstract — it is, precisely, "is PosSLP in P."** That question already has a name, a twenty-year research history, and a list of people who have tried hard to answer it. This is not a discouraging fact; it is a *locating* fact. It tells us exactly which mountain we are standing at the foot of.

### 3.3 The geometry tradition: separation bounds and exact predicates

A second, more applied tradition arrived at the same question from computational geometry, where one constantly needs to know the sign of a determinant or an orientation test built from input coordinates, and naïve full-precision evaluation is far too slow to use on every geometric predicate in, say, a Delaunay triangulation with millions of points.

**Definition 3.3 (Separation bound).** For an expression E with value ξ, a *separation bound* sep(E) is a positive rational number such that ξ ≠ 0 implies |ξ| ≥ sep(E).

Burnikel, Fleischer, Mehlhorn, and Schirra (*Algorithmica*, 2000), later extended by Burnikel, Funke, Mehlhorn, Schirra, and Schmitt (ESA 2001) into what is now called the **BFMSS bound**, gave explicit, efficiently computable separation bounds for expressions over {+, −, ×, ÷, k-th roots}, expressed purely in terms of the expression's *size* and *algebraic degree* — never in terms of the magnitude of its value. This bound is the engine inside the `real` number type of LEDA and the `Expr` type of the CORE library, both built specifically to let geometric algorithms compare expressions "natively," exactly as the note envisions.

Independently, Shewchuk's *Adaptive Precision Floating-Point Arithmetic and Fast Robust Geometric Predicates* (Discrete & Computational Geometry, 1997) built the now-standard technique used in essentially every production-grade computational geometry library: evaluate cheaply in ordinary floating point first; only if the result is too close to call, escalate to higher precision, using exactly enough extra precision — determined by a separation-bound-style error analysis — to resolve the sign. The running time of these predicates is *adaptive*: cheap when the compared values are far apart, expensive only when they are pathologically close.

This is, concretely, an existing, working, widely deployed instance of "expression-native comparison that avoids full scalar computation" — not a hypothetical. The remainder of this paper builds a formal space, the **Relational Order Space**, that generalizes this exact machinery and states precisely what it can and cannot guarantee.

## 4. Generalizing the Stress Test: Why No Representation Escapes the Information Floor

The note already performed the single most important diagnostic step itself, with the unary stress test (C = 1000 × 1000 vs. unary blow-up). It is worth showing that this is not a defect specific to unary notation — it is a special case of a general phenomenon that *any* candidate Relational Order Space must respect.

### 4.1 The squaring tower

Consider the tiny expression family:

> e₀ = 2,  e_{i+1} = e_i × e_i

After k multiplication operations, e_k = 2^(2^k). Writing this number down in binary requires about 2^k bits. The *expression* has size O(k); the *value* it denotes requires exponentially many bits in k to write down at all, in any positional base, let alone unary.

This is the note's own C = 1000 × 1000 example, iterated: a single squaring step already produced a million-fold blow-up in the unary stress test; a few dozen further squarings produce numbers with more digits than there are atoms in the observable universe, from an expression with a few dozen nodes.

**This is also why the note is correct that traditional scalar evaluation is not really O(n).** An evaluator that materializes the full value at every node pays, in the worst case, time exponential in circuit size — not because the *algorithm* is inefficient, but because the *output* it is being asked to materialize is exponentially large. This is precisely the phenomenon that motivated PosSLP as an object of study in the first place: complexity theorists separate "circuit size" from "bit-length of the computed integer" because conflating them — as ordinary scalar pipelines implicitly do — silently hides an exponential cost.

### 4.2 The information floor

The squaring tower generalizes the unary lesson into a quantitative statement: **any scheme that resolves a comparison must, in the worst case, account for as many bits as the smallest possible nonzero gap between two distinct values of the given expression size could be** — because if the true gap is that small, nothing short of resolving it can tell the two values apart. The BFMSS-style separation bound (Definition 3.3) is precisely the *best known explicit quantification* of how small that gap can be, as a function of size and degree alone; it is typically exponential in size for adversarially constructed expressions. This is not a flaw in BFMSS — it is a property of the mathematics, and BFMSS is the tightest known certificate of it.

Unary representation is simply the *most wasteful possible* way of paying this cost: it pays the full bit-length of the *value* even when the comparison did not require it. Binary scalar evaluation pays the cost more efficiently, but still pays it in full whenever the algorithm insists on fully evaluating before comparing. The constructive contribution below (§5) is to pay this cost **only when the structure of the problem proves it is unavoidable**, and not otherwise — which is the most any method can do.

### 4.3 Equality is the hard core

The information floor of §4.2 is at its most extreme exactly when val(A) = val(B): there the "gap" is zero, no finite precision will ever separate the two values, and the only way to be certain is an *exact* zero-test — which is, by Theorem 3.2, PosSLP-equivalent. This is the precise, formal version of the note's own diagnosis that "if we count the unary 1's and turn it into a scalar, the whole exercise becomes vacuous": any procedure that handles the equality case for free, in general, for all expressions, would be solving PosSLP for free, which is not known to be possible and is widely doubted.

## 5. The Relational Order Space (ROS): A Rigorous Construction

We now build the strongest framework that is actually achievable, honestly bounded by §3–4.

### 5.1 Formal definition

A **Relational Order Space** over a set of expressions ℰ consists of:

1. **A shared DAG layer.** Expressions are stored as arithmetic circuits with maximal subexpression sharing (Definition 1.1). This directly satisfies the note's "structural integrity" requirement: no expression is ever decomposed into a bare scalar at this layer.
2. **An interval annotation.** Each node v carries a verified rational interval [lo(v), hi(v)] guaranteed to contain val(v), computed bottom-up by interval arithmetic. This is the formal vehicle for the note's "distance without an explicit coordinate system": a verified *enclosure* of the value, rather than the value itself.
3. **A separation oracle.** A function sep(E) computable in time polynomial in |E| and the algebraic degree of E, satisfying Definition 3.3 (instantiated, e.g., by the BFMSS bound).
4. **A comparison certificate.** A proof object, returned alongside every "<", ">", or "=" verdict, consisting either of (a) two disjoint intervals witnessing the order (the cheap, common case), or (b) a precision level p ≥ sep(E) at which the intervals were refined until disjoint, or (c) in the equality case, an explicit algebraic identity (e.g., a common-denominator cancellation) witnessing val(A) − val(B) = 0.

### 5.2 Soundness and completeness

**Proposition 5.1 (Soundness).** Whenever ROS returns "A < B," "A = B," or "A > B," the corresponding relation holds between val(A) and val(B).

This is immediate from the interval invariant: intervals always contain the true value, so disjoint intervals cannot lie about order, and an explicit algebraic cancellation proof cannot lie about equality.

**Proposition 5.2 (Completeness).** For every pair A, B with val(A) ≠ val(B), ROS terminates and returns the correct order.

This follows because |val(A) − val(B)| ≥ sep(A − B) > 0 by Definition 3.3, so sufficiently refined intervals (any precision finer than sep(A−B)) are guaranteed to become disjoint. Termination in the equality case requires the explicit zero-test of layer 4(c), discussed in §5.4.

These two propositions are exactly the rigor the note asks for under "Comparability" and "Decidability" — but notice, per §2, that *establishing* them is not the hard part; the hard part, addressed next, is their *cost*.

### 5.3 Distance as verified enclosure

ROS does not produce |val(A) − val(B)| as a literal scalar (doing so would re-introduce the exponential blow-up of §4.1 whenever the true value is huge). Instead it produces a verified interval enclosing the difference, refined to whatever precision the calling application actually needs (e.g., "is the gap bigger than this threshold," which is itself just another instance of the Comparison Problem against a constant). This is the precise, non-vacuous content of the note's "distance or gap between expressions": a certified bracket, computed at a cost tied to how separated the values provably are, never at a cost tied to how large the values happen to be.

### 5.4 Complexity theorems

**Theorem 5.3 (Output-sensitive upper bound).** If A ≠ B and the BFMSS-style bound guarantees |val(A) − val(B)| ≥ 2^(−S) for S polynomial in the combined size and degree of A and B, then ROS determines the order in time polynomial in |A| + |B| + S — without ever expanding either value into its full canonical rational form.

This is the formal generalization of Shewchuk's adaptive-precision result and the BFMSS-driven LEDA/CORE number types (§3.3): cheap in the generic case, with a fully quantified, structure-driven (not value-driven) cost even in the expensive case, as long as the two values are genuinely distinct.

**Theorem 5.4 (Hardness of the residual case — restatement of Theorem 3.2).** Deciding the equality case (and, more generally, deciding order when the true gap is smaller than every efficiently-computable separation bound) is polynomial-time equivalent to PosSLP. No known technique — including the interval/separation-bound machinery of this section — resolves this case in less than PosSLP-equivalent cost, and no unconditional sub-PosSLP algorithm is known for it.

Together, Theorems 5.3 and 5.4 are the complete, honest answer to the note's "Computational Trade-Off" section: **ROS buys exactly the trade described there — generality (handling every pathological, possibly-equal case for free) is exchanged for decidability and efficiency on every case where the values are genuinely, certifiably distinct.** The boundary of that trade is not a design choice internal to ROS; it is externally fixed by Theorem 3.2, and pushing it further would be a result in complexity theory, not in this framework.

## 6. Self-Check: Stress-Testing ROS Against the Note's Own Examples

As requested, here is a deliberate adversarial run-through, to confirm the framework degrades exactly where the theory says it should, and nowhere else.

**Test 1 — the original C/D pair.** C = 1000 × 1000, D = 10⁶ + 1. Both circuits have size O(1). The interval layer evaluates each to a tight enclosure ([1000000, 1000000] and [1000001, 1000001] respectively) in O(1) time — no million-symbol unary string is ever built. The intervals are disjoint immediately, so ROS reports C < D at essentially the cost of ordinary floating-point evaluation. *Outcome: the unary blow-up identified in the note is fully avoided; this case sits entirely inside the cheap regime of Theorem 5.3.*

**Test 2 — the squaring tower (§4.1), 60 levels deep.** Both circuits have size ~60. Their true values have on the order of 2⁶⁰ bits. ROS never materializes these values. It computes a BFMSS-style separation bound directly from the circuit size and degree (polynomial in 60), and — since the two towers are constructed to differ at the very first squaring — the interval layer separates them after a handful of refinement steps, at low working precision, despite the values themselves being astronomically large. *Outcome: cost tracks circuit size, exactly as Theorem 5.3 promises, and exactly as the note's "far superior to scalar computation" intuition demands — for the generic, separated case.*

**Test 3 — an engineered exact tie.** A = (10⁶ + 1) − 1, B = 10⁶ × 1. Both denote exactly 1,000,000. Here interval refinement never separates the two, no matter how much precision is added — correctly so, since they are equal. ROS escalates to the exact layer (5.1.4c), finds the algebraic cancellation, and certifies equality. *Outcome: correctly handled, but only because this particular cancellation is easy to spot; Theorem 5.4 guarantees that an adversary who hides an equality inside a much larger, deliberately obfuscated circuit can force this layer to cost as much as solving PosSLP. This is the one case where ROS makes no efficiency promise beyond what is already known for PosSLP — and no other framework can do better without a complexity-theoretic breakthrough.*

**Test 4 — division by a near-zero denominator.** A = 1 / (10⁻³⁰ − 10⁻³⁰ + ε) for tiny symbolic ε. The denominator-clearing step (§3.2) moves the division to the top before any comparison is attempted, so ROS never silently divides by an interval that contains zero; if the denominator's sign cannot be certified, ROS correctly reports "undefined / requires refinement" rather than a wrong answer. *Outcome: soundness (Proposition 5.1) is preserved; this confirms the well-formedness caveat noted in Definition 1.3.*

No case was found, nor is one expected to exist, in which ROS is unsound. The only case in which it is not efficient is exactly the case Theorem 5.4 predicts.

## 7. What This Buys You: Real-World Deployments

It is worth being concrete about the practical value, since it is substantial and already realized, not speculative:

- **Computational geometry kernels** (CGAL, LEDA, CORE) use exactly this filter → separation-bound → refine architecture to make orientation tests, in-circle tests, and intersection predicates run at floating-point speed in the overwhelming majority of cases, falling back to certified exact arithmetic only on the genuinely ambiguous inputs. This is the single most important robustness technique in modern geometric and CAD software.
- **Root isolation and computer algebra systems** use the same separation-bound machinery to compare algebraic numbers — e.g., to sort the real roots of two polynomials — without computing high-precision decimal expansions of irrational numbers.
- **Symbolic-numeric hybrid systems** (motion planning, robotics, mesh generation) rely on these techniques specifically because Euclidean distance comparisons routinely involve square roots and sums that are exactly the territory of the sum-of-square-roots problem mentioned alongside PosSLP in the literature (§3.1).

These are not hypothetical extensions of the note's idea — they are existence proofs that the *non-degenerate* part of the note's vision is not only correct but already industrially deployed, with the engineering payoff (frequently 10–100× speedups over naïve exact arithmetic in geometric workloads) well documented in the cited sources.

## 8. What This Does Not Buy You: Re-Examining the Primary Hypothesis

The note's Primary Hypothesis states: "ALL contemporary important problems do not fundamentally require scalar decomposition." Having built the strongest available version of the framework, it is now possible to say precisely where this overreaches, and where it does not:

1. **Problems that are pure comparison/decision problems** — sorting, predicate evaluation in geometry, symbolic simplification, satisfiability of polynomial sign conditions — are exactly the class ROS targets, and for this class the hypothesis is largely *defensible*: scalar decomposition is frequently an avoidable, wasteful intermediate step, and the literature above already avoids it in practice.
2. **Problems that require scalar values for further arithmetic combination, physical interpretation, or numerical output** — physics simulation, machine learning weight updates, financial computation, anything that must eventually be displayed, summed across heterogeneous quantities, or fed into more arithmetic — cannot be replaced by an ordering-and-distance structure alone, because the *consumer* of the value needs the value, not merely its rank among other expressions. A Relational Order Space answers "which is bigger and by a certified bracket of how much"; it does not answer "what is 0.3 × this voltage," which is a different question requiring the scalar.
3. **Even within the comparison-only class, the hypothesis that this is unconditionally "far superior to O(n)"** holds only in the generic, well-separated regime of Theorem 5.3. In the degenerate regime, it is exactly as expensive as PosSLP — not "free," and not provably cheaper than scalar evaluation, because Theorem 3.2 shows the two costs are tied together by a polynomial-time equivalence.

So the honest restatement of the Primary Hypothesis is: *a large and practically important class of problems — comparison-heavy, decision-heavy problems — gain a real, already-realized advantage from relational/structural comparison over naïve scalar evaluation, with a precisely known efficiency ceiling set by PosSLP; this does not extend to all important problems, and does not remove the PosSLP ceiling even within the favorable class.*

## 9. Implementation Architecture on Classical Binary Hardware

The note asks explicitly whether such a system could run on ordinary 0/1 hardware through a new software/compiler layer. The answer is yes, and the architecture is already field-tested:

1. **Front-end / parser.** Build the expression DAG (Definition 1.1) with hash-consing so structurally identical subexpressions are physically shared — this alone often collapses exponential blow-up sources before any arithmetic happens.
2. **Filter layer.** Evaluate every node in ordinary IEEE-754 floating point, carrying a cheap running error bound. If the resulting interval for A − B does not straddle zero, return the comparison immediately. This is the fast path and handles the large majority of real workloads in O(circuit size) time, exactly as in Shewchuk's predicates.
3. **Certificate layer.** If the filter is inconclusive, compute a separation bound directly from the DAG's size and degree (no value computation involved) to determine the target precision that *would* resolve the comparison if the values are distinct.
4. **Refinement layer.** Re-evaluate using arbitrary-precision interval arithmetic up to the target precision from step 3. By Theorem 5.3, this terminates with a correct answer whenever val(A) ≠ val(B).
5. **Exact fallback layer.** Only if refinement never separates the intervals — the signal that val(A) may equal val(B) — fall back to exact rational arithmetic (common-denominator cross-multiplication) to certify equality outright. By Theorem 5.4, this is the only layer without a general sub-exponential guarantee, and it is reached rarely in practice precisely because true ties are measure-zero events among "generic" inputs.

This is a direct, buildable compiler/runtime layer sitting on top of ordinary binary arithmetic units — no new physical hardware is required, only the software discipline of never collapsing to a scalar earlier than layer 5 forces you to.

## 10. Research Program Going Forward

For anyone wanting to take this further, the open problems are now precisely locatable rather than vague:

- **Tightening separation bounds for restricted circuit classes** (e.g., bounded depth, bounded fan-in) is an active research area; any improvement directly widens the cheap regime of Theorem 5.3 without touching the PosSLP wall.
- **PosSLP ∈ P?** remains the central open question whose resolution would collapse the entire hierarchy of this paper into a single, unconditionally efficient algorithm. This is a major complexity-theory research target in its own right, studied by a small community for two decades; a serious attempt belongs in that literature, citing Allender–Bürgisser–Kjeldgaard-Pedersen–Miltersen (2006/2009) as the starting point.
- **Practical, domain-specific circuit structure** (e.g., expressions arising from a specific application domain, rather than worst-case adversarial circuits) may admit separation bounds far tighter than the general BFMSS bound — this is where genuinely new, useful mathematics is most likely to be found, and it is a tractable, non-open-problem-dependent research direction.

---

## Conclusion

The note's research question, taken seriously and made precise, turns out to be a real and important one: it is, exactly, the question of whether arithmetic-circuit sign determination (PosSLP) admits a polynomial-time algorithm. The note's own stress test correctly anticipated the central obstruction — that any representation paying for the full value rather than the structure will blow up — and this paper has shown that the obstruction generalizes far beyond unary notation into a structural fact about all such systems, with a precise name and a precise complexity-theoretic location.

What can be delivered, rigorously and without overclaiming, is the **Relational Order Space**: a DAG-based, interval-annotated, separation-bound-certified comparison framework that achieves exactly what the best existing science allows — structure-driven, value-independent cost whenever two expressions are genuinely distinct, with the cost ceiling collapsing to PosSLP-equivalence precisely and only at the boundary of equality. This is not a lesser result than the note hoped for; it is the same vision, given a name, a proof, and a working implementation already running inside the geometry and computer-algebra software the world relies on — together with an honest, citable map of exactly where the unsolved territory begins.
